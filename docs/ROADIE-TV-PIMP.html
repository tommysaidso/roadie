1 · The Position

ROADIE is a returning factual series built on infrastructure that is already running. The eight-ring route network, the venue and band registry, the booking and settlement platform, the advertising production arm and the interactive AI layer all exist and operate today under UARX MIXEDREALITY CORP. This package does not ask an investor to fund the construction of a business. It asks them to fund the camera pointed at one that is already moving.

The order is sixteen hours — two full seasons — produced end to end as one continuous campaign across the entire network rather than as two separate productions. One crew, one post pipeline, one mobilisation. That is why sixteen finished hours land under a million dollars, and why the second season carries no start-up cost whatsoever.

The shape of a season

There are eight rings, and each ring gets its own band. Eight bands go out per season, one per corridor, running their ring end to end. One episode is one ring and one band, so a season is eight hours and two seasons are sixteen. Sixteen bands over the run, all of them Canadian, most of them touring for the first time.

A ring is five to twelve towns. The band plays every stop on it; the camera unit covers three of those nights per ring and the advance days around them, which is what makes forty-eight filmed stops across two seasons fit inside sixty-two shoot days. In every filmed town the crew sells the night to the main street, and every business that says yes gets its commercial written and shot that day, on camera, as part of the episode. Then the band plays, and the door split is counted on camera: sixty percent band, twenty-five percent host venue, fifteen percent platform.

The ring finds its own cast

The series does not cast next season in an office. It finds it on the road. Every night on every ring has a local opener — a band from that town, playing in front of the crew, on a professional stage, often for the first time. Across a single ring that is five to twelve local acts auditioning without anyone calling it an audition. One of them gets a ring of their own next season.

This is a thread through every episode that pays off in the finale, and it costs nothing extra to shoot: the crew is already in the room, the stage is already up, and the tape is already rolling. It also means the stakes in the opening slot are real rather than manufactured. A band's entire year turns on twenty-five minutes in a Legion in Fort Macleod.

The handoff — and why nobody competes to stay

Season two takes a completely fresh set of eight bands. Nobody is carried over as a regular, and no band ever defends a place. The promise the format runs on is that the ring is open: a band who has never toured can be on it next year. A returning act defending territory would quietly kill that promise, and it would import the elimination machinery — judges, losers, a results show — that this series exists to avoid.

What carries over instead is the handoff. A season-one band returns at the top of season two, plays the first night of the new band's ring, and names the local opener they believe earned a ring. They have been through it, so their word means something the way a stranger's would not.

That single scene does three jobs at once. It is the check-in — we find out what actually happened to them, the record they did or did not make, the day job they did or did not quit, and it is deliberately not guaranteed to be a happy answer. It is the succession — the cast is chosen on camera by someone with standing to choose. And it is the emotional close of the season when the device repeats at the finale, bracketing the new cast with the last one.

The handoff is written inside the season-two premiere and finale at no additional episode cost. It can be struck as a standalone seventeenth hour if a broadcaster wants a special, and the budget carries the coverage to do so.

The format is self-feeding. Season one's towns produce season two's cast, season two's band hands to season three, and the registry behind the platform grows with every stop. A returning order therefore needs no new development spend and no casting call — the discovery is a by-product of the tour that is happening anyway.

2A · Rates, Reserves and Margin

Costed at union scale, whether or not we are signatory

Every labour line above is budgeted at the applicable scale rate. There is no single industry-wide rate; three agreements govern different people on this production:

  • UBCP/ACTRA — on-camera performers, which on this series means the host and any engaged on-camera contributor. Factual and documentary tiers apply.
  • SAG-AFTRA — carried as a provision only, against the possibility of a US performer. See the note below.
  • IATSE Local 891 — British Columbia crew. The field unit is costed at technician scale plus fringes, not at a flat day rate.
  • AFM — musicians, covering performance capture and any use of a recorded performance beyond the episode.
Why budget at scale when the production may not be signatory. Because it is the only number that cannot be argued down later. Scale is a published, third-party benchmark: it absorbs wage inflation across a two-season shoot, it survives a financier's or a bond company's review without a line-by-line defence, and if the production does go signatory mid-stream — which a broadcast licence can force — nothing has to be reopened. Costing below scale and hoping is how a budget becomes a deficit in year two.

Tax credits, rebates and program funding are treated throughout this package as post-delivery recovery, never as production cash flow. No shoot day depends on their arrival and no rate is set on the assumption of them.

Performer status — noted for the record

This is a Canadian production, made by a Canadian company, shot in British Columbia and Alberta with Canadian crew and Canadian performers. The operator participates in it as a Canadian, and the certification, credit and labour positions in this package all follow from that.

For completeness: the creator is a long-standing SAG-AFTRA member, by background a stunt coordinator, with thirty-six years in the industry. He is not a signatory producer. SAG-AFTRA's Global Rule One is the mechanism that would engage if a US performer were brought onto the production, and the host line is budgeted at signatory scale with pension and health — CA$56,000 in fees and CA$12,000 in P&H — so the provision exists in the budget whether or not it is ever drawn on. That is a deliberate cushion, not a forecast.

No US performer engagement is contemplated in the current plan. Should one arise, the agreement tier and its interaction with CAVCO certification and BC FIBC eligibility are confirmed with Canadian production counsel at that point. The budget carries the cost either way, so the schedule is not exposed to the answer. A previous UBCP membership, now lapsed, is not relied on anywhere in this package.

Contingency is a reserve, not margin

The 10% contingency exists to absorb weather, access, equipment failure and the ordinary overruns of sixty-two days of mobile production. It belongs to the production, not to the company, and it is not counted as profit anywhere in this package. Unspent contingency at delivery is upside, and it is deliberately not modelled as such.

The bond

A completion bond at 3% guarantees delivery to the financiers and is a normal precondition of institutional money and most broadcast licences. Bond companies commonly rebate a portion where no claim is made. That rebate is not budgeted here. It is treated the same way as the tax credits: recovery if it comes, never a line the production is counting on.

The producer and administration fee

A 10% producer and administration fee is what the company earns for carrying the production: the office, the accounting, the legal and clearance work, the payroll and reporting obligations, the routing and platform operations, and the risk of running the thing. It is a standard line in any Canadian production budget and it is set at the conventional rate.

This is a business, not a contribution. The fee is earned on delivery whether or not the series returns a dollar downstream, and it sits above the direct cost of production rather than being taken out of it. Any budget without it is a company paying to work, which is not a model that survives a second season. Downstream revenue is upside on top of the fee, not a substitute for it.

3A · The Anchor Advertiser

The single move that makes this work is one company buying the entire route network across both seasons and adopting the series message as its own. Not one ring — all eight, both seasons, all sixteen bands. That company is not a sponsor with a logo on a poster. It is the reason sixteen bands get to tour at all, and the series says so on screen.

What CA$1,000,000 buys

  • Every ring, every stop, both seasons. Eight corridors, forty-eight towns, sixteen bands, named and credited in all sixteen episodes. The brand is on every night the network runs, whether a camera is there or not.
  • Forty-eight commercials, produced and owned. One shot in each filmed town for their own location, dealer or franchisee in the market that location actually sells into — each delivered as a master they keep and run anywhere, in perpetuity.
  • Category exclusivity across the whole network for the full two-season term. No competitor appears anywhere on any ring.
  • The message. Their position carried as the civic spine of the show — small towns, working people, main streets that still have a main street — stated by the series rather than claimed by the brand.
  • Measurement. Per-stop reporting on the live ROI board: impressions, coupon pulls, gate attribution, by town and by ring.
Why it is cheap for them and decisive for us. CA$1,000,000 is a modest national campaign line, and it ordinarily buys thirty-second placements that stop existing the moment the flight ends. Here it buys forty-eight owned commercials at roughly eleven thousand dollars a town, sixteen hours of television in which the brand is the reason the thing happened, and a documented record of showing up in two provinces' worth of communities that national advertisers otherwise reach with a billboard. The brand gets an authenticity position that cannot be bought in a media plan. The series gets made. Neither side is doing the other a favour, which is why this closes.

Target profile

The fit is a brand whose customers are already in these towns and whose story is service, work or the road. The requirement is not budget size — CA$1,000,000 is a rounding error for any of these categories. It is that the message this series already tells is one they would want to be caught telling.

3B · The Micro Version — One Ring

This package models the full build: eight rings, two seasons, sixteen bands. The format also scales down without changing shape, and that version should be on the table in any conversation where the full commitment is the obstacle rather than the idea.

One ring, one band, one longer episode. A single corridor of five to twelve towns cut as one piece rather than a season, run a few minutes past a broadcast hour to compensate for the missing scale — and the extra room used the way the big version cannot afford to: stay in the diner longer, let an ad shoot go wrong on camera, play the whole song.

It is not a pilot and not a proof of concept. It is the same show at one-eighth the commitment. Everything structural holds — the commercials are made inside the episode, the door is counted on camera, the local openers are scouted, and the interactive layer works identically over one ring as over eight.

5 · Why the Second Season Costs Nothing to Start

Season one buys assets that season two does not repurchase: the routed network and its venue relationships, the advertiser list in forty-eight towns, the indexed footage library that powers the interactive layer, and the production and post pipeline itself. Because both seasons are shot in one continuous campaign, there is no second mobilisation, no second crew-up, and no second round of venue and permit negotiation. Only the eight bands change, and they are found on camera during the season before, from a registry that is already built.

The advertising line meanwhile grows with renewals from businesses who have already watched their own spot run. A third season is therefore accretive on both sides of the ledger at once, which is unusual in factual television and is a direct consequence of the advertising model rather than a projection about audience.

6 · Differentiator — Generation A

Every episode carries an interactive layer. The viewer stops the show at any point and asks, and it answers in the context of the scene playing — identifying a person and rolling the behind-the-scenes footage of them, showing a room empty before the crowd arrived, playing a song in full. The layer reads the entire shot library from all forty-eight stops, not only the finished cut, so the series deepens with every episode delivered instead of ageing.

On a linear airing, members with the ROADIE application receive the interactive layer on their device synced to the broadcast clock. This ties app membership directly to the television product and gives a broadcaster a measurable second-screen engagement asset no competing factual series currently offers.

8 · Live Ledger and Reporting

The operator runs settlement on its own platform. Door revenue splits sixty percent band, twenty-five percent host venue, fifteen percent platform, with the band guarantee escrowed before each convoy departs. Every advertising placement, coupon pull and gate figure is tracked per stop and per ring and is visible to advertisers and financiers on the live ROI board.

For this package that means committed, actual and variance lines are reported from the same system that runs the business, not assembled for a report. An investor, a broadcaster and the anchor advertiser all see the same numbers the operator sees, on the same day.

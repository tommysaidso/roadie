# ROADIE — one sheet

**Working title:** ROADIE
**Format:** Returning docu-series — 16 x 44 min, two full seasons
**Scale:** 8 rings · 48 stops · 16 bands · British Columbia and Alberta
**Producer:** UARX MIXEDREALITY CORP · EV Productions
**Budget:** CA$1,050,000 end to end — union scale, bonded, fee intact

---

## Logline

Eight bands nobody has heard of each get a touring circuit through the towns the
big tours drive past — and in every town, the show shoots the commercials for the
businesses on the main street. The ads you see in the break were made in the
episode you are watching.

---

## What it is, in one line

**A road show where the advertising is the story.**

Not a competition. Not a clip show. Not a talking-head music doc.

---

## The shape

There are **eight rings** — corridors of five to twelve towns each, already routed
across BC and Alberta. **Each ring gets its own band.** Eight bands go out per
season, one per corridor, running their ring end to end.

One episode is one ring and one band. A season is eight hours. Two seasons is
sixteen, and sixteen Canadian bands, most of them touring for the first time.

## The engine (why there is an episode 2)

Every stop runs the same clock:

1. **Roll in.** Convoy hits a town. Nobody has been here. Nothing booked past the room.
2. **The deal.** The crew sells the night to the main street — the hardware store,
   the credit union, the diner. Each one that says yes gets a commercial shot that day.
3. **The shoot.** We make their ad. Their words, their counter, their staff. One take,
   no agency. This is the comedy and the heart of the show.
4. **The night.** The band plays. 100 to 400 people in a Legion or a hall.
5. **The count.** Door split on camera. 60 band / 25 venue / 15 ROADIE.

The jeopardy is real and it is not manufactured: **will the town show up.** That
question answers itself on camera every episode, and the answer is different every time.

## The break is the show

Standard broadcast: the show stops, unrelated ads play, the show resumes.

ROADIE: the ad break is **content from the episode**. The spot for Trail Auto Body
that we watched get made in act two runs in the break, in market, and the owner
keeps the master forever.

- Nobody else on television can do this. A national broadcaster's ad cost rises with
  every market; ours does not, because the crew is already there.
- It makes the show fundable from local and national advertising budgets that already
  exist, rather than from a broadcast licence alone.
- It is the answer to "what is it?" — **a show that makes its own commercials and pays
  for itself in the towns it shoots in.**

## The ring finds its own cast

The series does not cast next season in an office. **It finds it on the road.**

Every night on every ring has a local opener — a band from that town, on a
professional stage, often for the first time. Across one ring that is five to twelve
local acts auditioning without anyone calling it an audition. One of them gets a ring
of their own next season.

It costs nothing extra to shoot: the crew is in the room, the stage is up, the tape is
rolling. And the stakes in the opening slot are real rather than manufactured. A band's
entire year turns on twenty-five minutes in a Legion in Fort Macleod.

## The handoff — and why nobody competes to stay

Season two takes a completely fresh set of eight bands. Nobody is carried over as a
regular and no band ever defends a place. The promise the format runs on is that the
ring is open: a band who has never toured can be on it next year. A returning act
defending territory would quietly kill that promise, and it would import the
elimination machinery — judges, losers, a results show — that this series exists to
avoid.

What carries over is **the handoff**. A season-one band returns at the top of season
two, plays the first night of the new band's ring, and names the local opener they
believe earned a ring. They have been through it, so their word means something a
stranger's would not.

That one scene does three jobs:

- **The check-in** — what actually happened to them. The record they did or did not
  make, the day job they did or did not quit. Deliberately not guaranteed to be a
  happy answer.
- **The succession** — the cast is chosen on camera by someone with standing to choose.
- **The close** — repeated at the finale, it brackets the new cast with the last one.

**The format is self-feeding.** Season one's towns produce season two's cast. A
returning order needs no development spend and no casting call — discovery is a
by-product of the tour that is happening anyway.

## The chairs

**The Host.** Rides the whole ring. Carries the cold open, does the interviews, holds
a broadcaster's confidence. The seat is the format; the name is casting — it can be a
working television host, or it can be the ring operator himself, which is the cheaper
and in some ways more honest version.

**The Veteran.** A legacy Canadian act who has played these rooms for forty years.
**The veteran does not tour.** One block day — a chair, a camera, two or three hours —
cut across the whole season. Before a band walks into a room, we cut to someone who
played it in 1983 and knows what the load-in is like and what the promoter will try.
Then we watch them find out. He is right sometimes and wrong sometimes, and both are
good television. He never judges and never eliminates. A blessing, not a job.

**The Rookie.** The band on that ring. Never toured. Gets the guarantee, the room, and
the chance to be wrong in front of a camera.

## Generation A — the interactive layer

Watch it and talk to it. At any point the viewer stops the episode and asks, and the
show answers in the context of the scene playing:

- "Who is that guy?" — it tells you, and rolls the behind-the-scenes clip of him.
- "What did that room look like empty?" — it shows you.
- "Play the whole song." — it plays the whole song.

The AI reads the entire footage library from all forty-eight stops, not just the cut,
so the show gets deeper every week instead of older.

**Broadcast sync:** when the episode airs linear, members with the ROADIE app get the
interactive layer on their phone, synced to the broadcast clock. Show on the TV, show
in your hand.

No returning series has done this. It is a format category, not a feature.

## The micro version — one ring

The eight-ring build is the plan. The format also scales down without changing shape, and
that version belongs in any conversation where the size of the commitment is the obstacle
rather than the idea.

**One ring, one band, one longer episode.** A single corridor of five to twelve towns cut
as one piece rather than a season — run a few minutes past a broadcast hour to compensate
for the missing scale, and use the extra room the way the big version cannot afford to:
stay in the diner longer, let an ad shoot go wrong on camera, play the whole song.

It is not a pilot and not a proof of concept. It is the same show at one-eighth the
commitment. Everything structural holds — the commercials are made inside the episode, the
door is counted on camera, the local openers are scouted, and the interactive layer works
identically over one ring as over eight.

|  | Full build | Micro |
|---|---|---|
| Rings | 8 | 1 |
| Bands | 16 | 1 |
| Finished hours | 16 x 44 | 1 x 52–60 |
| Filmed stops | 48 | 5–8 |
| Advertising | CA$1,000,000 | CA$150,000 |
| Budget | CA$1,050,000 | CA$170,000 |

A brand that will not commit CA$1,000,000 to a network it has never seen will often commit
CA$150,000 to one corridor. The micro version turns a single no into a first ring — and a
first ring that works is the only argument the full build ever really needs.

## The money

**CA$1,050,000 for sixteen hours — CA$65,625 a finished hour.** Materially below the
Canadian factual mid-tier, and costed at union scale rather than talked down to a number.

| | CAD |
|---|---|
| Direct cost of production | 852,000 |
| Contingency (10% of direct) | 85,000 |
| Completion bond (3%) | 28,000 |
| Producer and administration fee (10% of direct) | 85,000 |
| **Total** | **1,050,000** |

Contingency, bond and fee sit **on top of** direct cost, never inside it. The contingency is a
reserve and belongs to the production, not the company. The fee is what the company earns for
carrying the thing — office, accounting, legal and clearance, payroll, routing and platform
operations, and the risk of running it — and it is earned on delivery whether or not a dollar
comes back downstream. A budget without it is a company paying to work.

The reason the number holds at this level: the rings tour on their own economics, so the
series budget carries the camera department, not the tour.

**The primary ask is not equity.** It is one national advertiser taking the entire network
across both seasons at CA$1,000,000. That, plus the tax credits it generates and a Canadian
broadcast licence, finances all sixteen hours with no equity, no debt, no cap table and
nothing to recoup. Rebates and credits are treated as post-delivery recovery throughout —
never as production cash flow. Full modelling is in the PIMP package.

## Why not the other formats

| Considered | Why not |
|---|---|
| X-Factor / Making the Band | Competition needs judges, elimination, and a cast of 30. It makes the band the product and the town the backdrop. Every broadcaster already has one. |
| MTV-style clip show | No spine, no reason to watch two. Music blocks are a playlist, not a series. |
| Interview documentary | Every music doc is this. It does not use the one thing we have that nobody else has: we are actually going there. |

Closest honest comparisons: **Bourdain** (place-led), **Amazing Race** (the route is
the engine), **Long Way Round** (the convoy is the cast) — with a music payoff at the
end of every hour.

1 · The Series

Eight bands who have never toured each get a circuit of small towns — the places national tours drive past. One band per route, one route per episode. They arrive with nothing booked past the room, they have to make the town care, and at the end of the night the door money is counted on camera in front of everyone.

The jeopardy is not manufactured and there is no elimination: the question every hour asks is will the town show up, and it answers itself on camera. Sometimes the hall is full and sometimes it is forty people and a bartender, and both are the show.

The self-feeding format

Every night has a local opener — a band from that town, on a professional stage, often for the first time. Across a season that is dozens of local acts auditioning without anyone calling it an audition, and one of them gets a route of their own next season.

Season two therefore casts itself on camera during season one. A returning band comes back to hand over: they play the first night of the new band's route, they tell you honestly what the year did to them, and they name the opener they think earned it. No judges, no elimination, no results show — and no development spend or casting call for a renewal.

Why this matters to a commissioner. Most returnable unscripted needs a fresh casting process, a fresh location deal and a fresh development cycle every year. This one generates next season's cast, next season's towns and next season's stories as a by-product of shooting this one. The renewal is cheaper than the order.

2 · One Roll, Four Pieces

The crew rolls once in each town and comes away with four things. They are the same footage, the same people and the same night, so each one makes the others cheaper and more real.

PieceWhat it is
1. The documentary The series itself. We sit down with the locals who are proud of the place and let them say how they feel about it, then follow the band: the night they play, and the life and back story that got them there. People want to play again and tell how they got here, and that is the hour.
2. The music video A music video with the band, shot in that town, with that town in it. The band leaves the route with something they can use for years.
3. The branded film For the paying advertiser or sponsor: a film shot in that community's backyards, built around a through-story or theme the brand wants to carry out of the localised shoot. The brand keeps the master. This is what funds the run.
4. Local Jams The community layer. Local Jams lives inside ROADIE and plugs into Social Jams, so each town stays playing and connected after the run moves on: the local openers, the jams, the next season's cast. The brand stays too. It is the one that brought the show to town, and the community remembers that long after the trucks leave.

The documentary is what a network buys. The branded film is what an advertiser buys. The music video is what the band takes home, and Local Jams is what the town keeps, with the brand still in it. One crew, one roll.

3 · Why It Travels

The format needs only three things: a corridor of small towns, a band nobody has heard of, and a room. Every one of those exists in every territory with a highway.

TerritoryStatusDetail
Canada — BC and AlbertaRouted and operating 8 rings, 48 stops, venue and band registry live, settlement platform running.
United States — WestRouted, dated, ready 3 rings and 20 stops across Washington, Oregon and California — Cascade (Bellingham to Eugene), Siskiyou (Roseburg to Sacramento), Valley (Stockton to Big Bear). 21 county fairgrounds and expo centres identified, dated for 2027.
Format territoriesAvailable Australia, New Zealand, Ireland, the UK, the Nordics and any market with a regional touring desert. Local band, local towns, the brands that sell into those towns, identical structure.

The US routes are not aspiration. They are seeded in the same platform that runs the Canadian network, against real fairgrounds and expo centres of the size the rig fits, with dates attached. A buyer taking the US version is commissioning something already mapped.

One order, or a global format

A buyer can take the Canadian series as finished programming, commission a domestic version of the format, or take both and cross-promote them. The episodes are territory-locked by design — the towns are the point — so a US series and a Canadian series never compete for the same audience story, and a global buyer can run a version per market from one bible.

4 · Generation A — The Interactive Layer

Every episode carries an interactive layer, built and running. The viewer stops the episode at any point and asks, and the show answers in the context of the scene playing — identifying a person and rolling the behind-the-scenes footage of them, showing what a room looked like before the crowd arrived, playing a song in full.

It reads the entire shot library rather than the finished cut, so the series deepens with every episode delivered instead of ageing. On a linear or scheduled airing it syncs to the broadcast clock on the viewer's own device: show on the screen, show in their hand.

This is a platform feature, not a marketing gimmick. For a buyer with a device ecosystem or a second-screen strategy, ROADIE is a working demonstration of a returning series that is also an interactive product — already built, already integrated, and exclusive to whoever takes the show. No competing factual series currently offers it.

5 · The Commercial Architecture

This series has a second business inside it. In every town the crew also shoots a branded film for the paying advertiser or sponsor, whether that is a brand on one town, a ring sponsor or one brand across the whole run. It is shot in that community's own backyards, with its people, around a through-story or theme the brand chooses, and the brand keeps the master. That work is paid for by the advertisers and it substantially funds the production.

How that reads depends entirely on the buyer. It is deliberately structured so it can be either the format's engine or an invisible financing mechanism:

Buyer typeHow the advertising is handled
Ad-supported streamer or broadcaster The commercials made inside the episode run in that episode's break, in market. The show supplies its own inventory and the break stops being an interruption. This is the strongest version of the format and it is unique to it.
Ad-free premium streamer A dedicated platform cut — see below. No commercial ever plays on their service; the scenes of the towns making them stay, because that is where the story is. To the buyer the advertising is simply the reason a fully-bonded, union-scale season costs a fraction of the category norm.
Co-production or format sale The advertising arm is carried by the local producer under licence, which is what makes a low-cost local version viable in a territory with a small commissioning budget.

Two cuts, one shoot

The series is shot once and finished two ways, and the difference between them is a recut rather than a reshoot.

CutWhat it contains
Ad-supported cut The full format. The commercial made in act two plays in that episode's break, in market. The show supplies its own inventory.
Platform cut — ad-free The finished commercials come out. Everything human about making them stays: the crew asking the town what it is proud of, the locals who have never been on camera, the brand's theme turning up in a real backyard, the moment it works. No advertising plays on the platform at any point.
Why this costs almost nothing to deliver. The finished spots are already separate deliverables — they are made for the advertiser to keep and run on their own channels, not assembled into the episode. Lifting them out is a trim, not a restructure, because the story was never the commercial. The story is a town saying, on camera, what it is proud of, and that scene plays identically on an ad-free platform.

Practically: an ad-free buyer takes a clean cut with no commercial content on their service, and the advertising business continues off-platform, unchanged for the towns and the advertisers. The buyer gets the show; nobody loses the ads.

The deficit case, stated plainly. A full two-season order — sixteen hours, sixteen bands, forty-eight towns — is budgeted at CA$1,050,000 at union scale, bonded, with a producer fee intact. Roughly CA$65,600 a finished hour. The advertising business and Canadian tax credits carry the majority of that, so the licence fee required from a buyer is a fraction of what an hour of this scope normally costs. Full modelling available on request.

6 · What We Are Asking For

  • A licence for the Canadian series — two seasons, sixteen hours, deliverable against a routed and operating network.
  • Or a domestic commission of the format — beginning with the US West rings, already routed and dated.
  • Or a development conversation about a global format roll-out from one bible.

What we are not asking for is development money to work out whether this is possible. The route network, the venue and band registry, the booking and settlement platform, the advertising production arm and the interactive layer are built and running today.

Ranked by fit, not by prestige. Fit here means: does the buyer's model let the format be what it actually is.

MTV / Paramount unscripted (US) Music-first unscripted with a discovery engine, at a budget that is a rounding error for them.

Tier 3 — aspirational, and honest about why

BuyerThe obstacle
Apple TV+ Originals The advertising objection is answered by the platform cut — Apple would take a clean, ad-free version with no commercial content on the service at all, and would never need to know or care that the ads exist. Two frictions remain. Its unscripted slate is small, premium and talent-led, and this series is deliberately built around people nobody has heard of. And it does not accept unsolicited material — see access, below. The genuine hook for Apple is the interactive layer as a device and ecosystem story, and that is what any approach should lead with.

Netflix — a separate case

BuyerWhy it is not in Tier 3
Netflix unscripted Two things separate it from Apple. It carries an advertising tier, so the format's strongest idea — a break filled with commercials made inside the episode — has somewhere to live rather than being a contradiction. And rolling a format out as local versions per territory is its established unscripted playbook, which is exactly the shape of this show: two territories routed, a bible that localises to any market with a highway and a touring desert. The remaining obstacle is access, and where a warm internal introduction exists that obstacle is largely gone.
Access — the part nobody puts in a pitch document. Apple, Netflix and the major US streamers do not read unsolicited pitches, and will not open an email from an unrepresented producer. Material reaches them exactly three ways: a production company with an existing overall or first-look deal, a literary agent at one of the major agencies, or an entertainment lawyer with buyer relationships. Reaching that tier means attaching one of those three first, which is a separate and prior piece of work to any pitch meeting. A Tier 1 buyer, by contrast, can be approached directly today.

The practical sequence is therefore: land a Tier 1 order, shoot it, and let a finished season with an audience and a working interactive layer be the thing that opens the Tier 3 door. Attachment is cheap once something exists; it is nearly impossible before.

8 · What Changes If a Buyer Comes In

A commission is not the same business as the independent build, and it is better to say so at the start than to renegotiate it later. Five things move.

AreaWhat changes
Where the money comes from A licence fee displaces most or all of the network advertising anchor. The independent model funds the series from advertisers and recovers through credits; a commissioned model funds it from the buyer. The two do not simply add together, and the budget rises to a network delivery standard regardless.
The advertising business Most buyers will want some control over commercial content inside the programme. On an ad-free platform the commercials come off the show entirely and live only on the advertisers' own channels. That is survivable — the branded films still get made and the brands still keep their masters — but it removes the break-is-the-show idea from the finished episode, and with it a large part of the independent revenue line.
Rights A global streamer will ask for worldwide rights in perpetuity. That is the single most expensive thing to give away here, because it forecloses the format sale — the local-version business in other territories — which is the largest long-term value in the property. Territory-limited or windowed rights are worth arguing for even at a lower fee.
Cost and overhead Network delivery brings standards documentation, legal and clearance review, E&O at a higher tier, production insurance to their schedule, and approvals at every stage. Budget at the CA$1.05M level is an independent number; a commissioned version of the same sixteen hours costs materially more, and the fee has to cover it.
How it is run Creative approvals on casting, cuts and structure, a delivery schedule set by them rather than by the ring's touring calendar, and a production reporting obligation. The rings currently move on their own economics; a commissioned season has to move on the broadcaster's.
None of this is an argument against a commission. A buyer brings reach, a marketing budget, and a credential the independent version cannot buy. It is an argument for going into the room already knowing which terms are negotiable and which are not — and for treating worldwide format rights as the one thing not casually given away.
